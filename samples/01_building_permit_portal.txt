Stakeholder interview: Online building permit portal
Municipality of Seeberg (fictional), Canton of St. Gallen, Building Department
Participants: Interviewer (Product Owner), Monika Keller (Head of Building Department), Daniel Rossi (Permit Clerk), Sandra Frei (IT Coordinator)
Note: Synthetic transcript created for a portfolio project. The municipality, people and statements are fictional.

Interviewer: Thanks for making time. Could you start by describing how a building permit application works today?

Monika Keller: Sure. Today everything comes in on paper. The applicant, or usually their architect, sends us the application form plus plans in three printed copies. We stamp it, Daniel checks it for completeness, and then it goes around to the different offices: fire protection, environment, sometimes the canton.

Daniel Rossi: And the completeness check is where most of my time goes. I'd say six out of ten applications are missing something. A signature, the neighbour consent, the wrong plan scale. Then I write a letter, we wait two weeks, and the clock starts again.

Interviewer: What would you like to change?

Monika Keller: Our goal is that applications can be submitted fully online. The council wants to show progress on digitalisation this year, and honestly we are running out of space for the paper archive.

Daniel Rossi: For me the biggest win would be if the system told the architect what is missing before they submit. If the form simply doesn't let them submit without the neighbour consent, I save hours every week.

Interviewer: What documents are usually required?

Daniel Rossi: The application form, site plan, floor plans, sections and elevations, the neighbour consent if it's close to the property line, and for larger projects an energy certificate. Plans come as PDF from the architects anyway. Some files are huge, 200 megabytes is not unusual.

Sandra Frei: That's something to keep in mind. Our current file server isn't made for that, and we can't just put everything into some cloud in America. Data has to stay in Switzerland, that's a clear requirement from the canton's data protection office.

Interviewer: Who submits applications? Mainly architects?

Monika Keller: About 80 percent come from architects or builders. But we also have private homeowners who want to build a garden shed or install solar panels. They're often older and not very comfortable with computers.

Daniel Rossi: That's why I think we should still accept paper. If Mr. Müller, who is 78, comes to the counter with his application, I'm not going to send him away.

Monika Keller: I see that differently. If we keep both channels forever, we never get rid of the double work. Maybe the counter staff scan it in for him, but the process behind it should be digital only.

Interviewer: So the question is whether paper submissions remain possible, and if so, who digitises them?

Monika Keller: Yes, that needs to be decided. I'd have to discuss it with the council.

Interviewer: What happens after submission?

Daniel Rossi: The applicant wants to know where their application is. Right now they call us. I get maybe ten calls a day just asking "what's the status?" If they could see the status online, that would help a lot.

Monika Keller: But we need to be careful what we show. If the fire protection office has concerns, we don't want the applicant to see internal comments before we've discussed them.

Interviewer: So applicants should see the status, but not internal comments.

Monika Keller: Exactly. A status like "received", "in review", "additional documents needed", "decision made". That's enough.

Interviewer: How do the other offices get involved today?

Daniel Rossi: We copy the plans and send them by internal mail. Fire protection takes on average three weeks. It would be great if they could review directly in the system and leave their statement there.

Sandra Frei: The fire protection office is part of the regional authority, not the municipality, so they'd need external access. That means we need proper login and roles. And every access to a dossier should be logged, because these are legal proceedings. If there's an appeal, we must be able to show who saw and changed what.

Interviewer: Are there any requirements about languages?

Monika Keller: Seeberg is German-speaking, but we have many international residents who work for companies in the St. Gallen region. The council wants the portal in German and English.

Sandra Frei: And accessibility. Public websites have to meet the federal accessibility standard. We had complaints last year about our website not working with screen readers.

Interviewer: What about the decision itself?

Monika Keller: The decision is a formal ruling, signed by the council. Today it's sent by registered mail. In the future it should be possible to deliver it electronically, but only if the applicant agrees to electronic delivery. Legally that's important.

Interviewer: How long do you have to keep the files?

Sandra Frei: Building files are kept for the life of the building, basically forever. Other documents like correspondence, I think ten years, but I'd need to check with the archive.

Interviewer: Is there a deadline for the project?

Monika Keller: The council wants a first version live by next summer. It doesn't need to do everything, but submission online and status tracking should be there. The integration with the canton can come later.

Daniel Rossi: And please, it has to be fast. If uploading plans takes forever, architects will just keep sending paper.

Interviewer: What would success look like for you in a year?

Monika Keller: Most applications come in online, Daniel spends less time chasing missing documents, and we get fewer phone calls.

Daniel Rossi: If I could cut the completeness letters in half, I'd be happy.

Interviewer: Thank you, this was very helpful. I'll come back with a first draft of the backlog and some questions.
